Story One

The Morning Prayer of the Saints

White with snow1In this story, the color white predominates, symbolizing the holiness of the three saints and the purity of the natural world. The narrative emphasizes that everything, including the saints themselves, is "covered with snow," with specific attention paid to the white elements of their humble attire: St. Nicholas's "frost-hardened sheepskin coat," St. Sergius's "skufia, white with snow" (a skufia being a clerical cap), and St. Seraphim's "white quilted… jacket," while "their white beards flutter in the wind." This whiteness, blanketing the Russian landscape, connects directly to the story's central theme: that Holy Russia, though presently "darkened with sins" and "stained with the blood" of the revolution, is ultimately "just a child...the Lord's gentle thought," who will eventually repent and be saved., St. Nicholas the Wonderworker, St. Sergius of Radonezh, and St. Seraphim of Sarov walk together through the spacious evening fields.2Three of the saints most beloved throughout all Russia are St. Nicholas the Wonderworker, St. Sergius of Radonezh, and St. Seraphim of Sarov.

The snowstorm is blowing up. The frost freezes the lonely snowy land.

"Why do I love Holy Russia?" replies Nicholas, looking into Sergius's eyes. "She is a child — Rus'!… A quiet, fragrant flower… a gentle thought of God18The original Russian phrase describing Russia is «Кроткая дума Господня» (Krotkaya duma Gospodnya). It is a spiritual ideal, referring to the belief that the Russian land and its people have a unique, God-bearing vocation focused on humility, faith, and piety, despite their sins and sufferings.

"You spoke well, Nicholas, about Rus'," whispered Seraphim quietly. "I want to get on my knees, my joys!20The phrase "my joy" is an authentic signature saying historically associated with the real St. Seraphim of Sarov. By having the literary St. Seraphim use this term for his fellow saints, the author emphasizes that their arduous journey through the bitterly cold night is, in essence, a pilgrimage illuminated by divine joy. "But, holy fathers,"21According to Eastern Orthodox tradition, the term "holy fathers" is a formal and reverential form of address with two key meanings. By using this term, St. Sergius is not merely being polite; he is acknowledging the shared, holy authority of St. Nicholas and St. Seraphim. Sergius asked timidly, "what about the years of blood in 1917, 1918, and 1919? Why did the Russian people stain themselves with blood?"22The years 1917, 1918, and 1919 are identified here as the "years of blood" because they mark the core period of the Russian Revolution and the ensuing Civil War. This span encompasses the Bolshevik seizure of power and the eruption of a full-scale civil war. For the story's saints, these years represent the profound national trauma where the Russian people "stained themselves with blood" through fratricidal conflict and revolutionary terror, requiring divine forgiveness and repentance.

"They will repent!" St. Nicholas the Wonderworker answered with conviction.

"They will be saved!" Seraphim said firmly.23"They will be saved!" — For the author and his original Russian audience, living through the cruel aftermath of the Bolshevik revolution, this statement from St. Seraphim of Sarov would have carried immense prophetic weight, transforming the dialogue from simple hope into a specific divine promise. Long before this story was written, St. Seraphim of Sarov (1754-1833) famously prophesied that Russia would endure a "terrible storm" of godless persecution — a prophecy fulfilled by the Bolshevik Revolution and the horrific bloodshed that came as a result. However, St. Seraphim also foretold that this suffering would not be the end. He promised a glorious resurrection of faith, declaring that a great repentance would occur and that God would lead Russia by the path of sorrows to great glory. Therefore, in the story, when St. Nicholas states, "They will repent!" it is the necessary human response to the tragedy. But when St. Seraphim firmly adds, "They will be saved!" it is the definitive prophetic answer from above. It is the assurance that God's mercy transcends the worst human sin, and that the nation's story, in the eyes of faith, does not end in the bloodstained years of the revolution, but in a triumphant, God-given redemption.

"Let us pray!" whispered Sergius. They reached a small, snow-covered forest church. In the darkness, they lit candles before the icons and began to serve matins – the morning prayer service.

Outside the church walls, the snowy Kitezh forest roared. The blizzard sang. The hierarchs24The term "hierarchs" specifically means "saintly bishops". And after matins, the three intercessors went out onto the porch of the church, and blessed the snowy land, the blizzard, and the night, unto the four corners of the earth.

Story Two

The Silver Blizzard1"The Silver Blizzard" is a poetic masterpiece that captures the essence of a Russian Christmas. Through the use of rich sensory imagery, a charming child's narrative voice, and profound religious feeling, Nikiforov-Volgin creates a world where the divine touches the everyday. The story isn't just about the holiday; it's about the consciousness of a child for whom the boundary between the spiritual and the physical is wonderfully blurred, making the entire world — from the frost on the window to a star in the sky — a part of the Christmas miracle.

There was nearly a month left until Christmas, but it was already upon you — blowing in with snowy dust, clinging to frosty windowpanes in the mornings, jingling with sleigh runners along the blue roads, singing, "Christ is born! Glorify Him!"2"Christ is born! Glorify Him!" is a hymn that is sung in Eastern Orthodox churches during the weeks up until Christmas. As a result, this phrase also reflects a traditional Orthodox Christmas greeting. One person says, "Christ is Born!" and the customary response is "Glorify Him!" This exchange is an Orthodox Christian's concise and joyful declaration of belief in the Incarnation. at the All-Night Vigil3The All-Night Vigil is a liturgical service in the Eastern Orthodox Church, celebrated on the eve of Sundays and major feast days like Christmas. While its name recalls ancient monastic traditions of all-night prayer, the service in a typical parish context usually lasts between two and three hours. It is a composite service that combines elements of Vespers, Matins, and the First Hour, and is designed to be a spiritual "vigil" or watch, preparing the faithful for the celebration of the Divine Liturgy the following morning. in church, and visiting your dreams at night as a merry silver blizzard.4The "silver blizzard" is the story's central metaphor. It operates on three levels: literally, it is the sparkling snowstorm of a Russian winter; emotionally, it represents the child's exhilarating anticipation of Christmas; and spiritually, it symbolizes the divine grace and transformative presence of the Christmas miracle, permeating and sanctifying the entire world.

During these days, you didn't want to think about anything earthly, especially school. My family noticed my pre-holiday mood and sternly declared:

"If you bring home bad grades from school, you can forget about the Christmas tree and new boots!"

"No matter," I thought, "we'll see… If they give me a three in conduct, as they threatened, I'll bring it up to a five… In arithmetic, they're sure to give me a two, but that's no trouble either.5The narrator is referring to the standard grading scale in Russian schools, which uses numbers from 2 to 5. His plan is to improve his poor grades ("2"s and "3"s) into perfect "5"s. Mikhál Vasílyich's twos always look like little swan necks, without a circle around them — I'll change that into a five too…"6The detail about the "two" resembling a "swan's neck" describes the teacher's distinctive handwriting. Unlike a printed "2" that might have a closed loop, this teacher writes it with an open, curved line. The narrator believes this flaw will make it easy to change the "2" into a "5" by adding a few strategic pen strokes.

Once I had figured all this out, I told my parents:

"I saw the Christmas star in the sky… A big, blue one!"

"You'll get it from me after the service!"

The silence in the church grew even quieter. Silanty raised his voice and distinctly, loudly, with an unexpected clarity for him, exclaimed:

"God is with us! Understand, all ye nations, and submit yourselves, for God is with us!"22This exclamation, sung with great power in the Eastern Orthodox Church during the Christmas service (in the Royal Hours or the Vigil), is taken from the prophecy of Isaiah (8:9-10, LXX). In the context of the story, the transformation of the reader Silanty's voice when he proclaims this shows the immense spiritual power of the phrase. It is the theological core of the Nativity: the all-powerful, transcendent God has now entered human history as a vulnerable child, Emmanuel, which literally means "God is with us."

The choir, with all the best voices in town, picked up his scattered words brightly and resoundingly:

"God is with us! Understand, all ye nations, and submit yourselves, for God is with us!"

The priest in white vestments opened the Royal Doors, and in the altar it was white, so white from the silver brocade on the altar table and the prothesis.23This passage describes a moment when the sanctuary, the most sacred part of an Orthodox church, is revealed during the Christmas service. Royal Doors: The central doors in the icon screen (iconostasis) that separates the nave from the altar. They are opened at key moments in the liturgy, symbolizing the opening of Heaven to humanity through Christ. Altar Table (Holy Table): The table in the center of the altar area upon which the Eucharist is celebrated. It is considered the Throne of God. Prothesis (Table of Preparation): A smaller table to the left of the altar table where the bread and wine are prepared before the Eucharist. Silver Brocade: A heavy, ornate fabric woven with silver threads, used to cover the altar and prothesis for major feasts like Christmas, creating a scene of brilliant, heavenly splendor.

When they finished singing this lofty hymn, they closed the Royal Doors, and Silanty began reading again. Now he read briskly and clearly, as if the song that had just faded had silvered his dull voice.

About the Author

Vasily Akimovich Nikiforov-Volgin
1900 – 1941

☩

Vasily Akimovich Nikiforov-Volgin, a unique Orthodox Christian writer, was born on December 24, 1900 (January 6, 1901, New Style) in the village of Markushi, Kalyazinsky Uyezd, in the Tver Province, on the Volga River, into a deeply religious peasant family. His father was a cobbler. Soon after his birth, the Nikiforov family moved to Narva (which was then part of the St. Petersburg Governorate).

After 1920, according to the Treaty of Tartu, the territory on the right bank of the Narova River (Narva with the Prinarovye region) was transferred to the Republic of Estonia. Consequently, the writer's family became involuntary emigrants, cut off from their dearly beloved Russian homeland. However, in the 1920s and 30s, the old Russian way of life, with its customs and traditions, order and practices, was still preserved there. It was this life that Nikiforov-Volgin described in his sketches and stories.

The future writer only managed to complete his studies at the parish school of the St. Vladimir Brotherhood. However, it was a good school. The young man engaged in serious self-education extensively. His passion for books and reading manifested itself in early childhood. Deeply religious from childhood and possessing a high-pitched voice with clear, expressive diction, he became a precentor* at the Holy Transfiguration Cathedral in Narva and remained one until the spring of 1932. This is the source of his excellent knowledge of church life and the clergy's way of life, which is evident in his works.

According to the recollections of one of his friends, Vasily loved to take a notebook and pencil and sneak into a secluded corner of the church watchhouse before the service began, listening to worshippers and people who just came in to warm up, and writing down what they were talking about among themselves. Their poignant remarks and folk expressions later found their way into his stories.

In 1923, he began his regular literary and journalistic activities. In 1927, he received his first award for writing: at a competition for young authors organized by the oldest Estonian literary association — The Literary Circle in Tallinn — first prize was awarded to V. Nikiforov-Volgin's story, "Bowing to the Ground." He usually signed his publications with the pseudonym Vasily Volgin — in memory of the great Russian river on which his native village was located. Over time, he began signing his name as V. Nikiforov-Volgin, combining his surname with his pseudonym.

By the mid-1930s, Nikiforov-Volgin was already known in the literary circles of the Russian Diaspora, corresponding with I. Shmelev, A. Amfiteatrov, B. Zaitsev, and others. In early 1936, he moved to Tallinn, becoming a home tutor and educator. Among his pupils was Alexy (Ridiger), the future Patriarch of Moscow and All Russia, Alexy II.

Two collections of his stories were published — "The Earth Celebrates Her Name Day" (1937) and "The Pilgrim's Staff" (1938), which were met with positive reviews in Russian publications abroad.

According to the recollections of those who knew him, Vasily Nikiforov was an exceptionally kind, warm-hearted, and responsive person. His sisters told how once, in severe frost, Vasily gave his hat and winter coat to a beggar on the street, justifying himself at home by saying: "I felt sorry for the man — he was freezing, and I'll manage somehow…"

Nikiforov-Volgin was a deeply religious man, and this primarily defined his worldview and the character of all his work. Memoir-stories about childhood constituted a significant part of his work. He depicted scenes of church services, church holidays, and a boy's communication with "holy people." Nikiforov-Volgin depicts this world of the church and believers — so familiar to him — accurately, authentically, and with love. He manages to recreate all the minutiae of this world in his works, including everyday details, and at the same time convey its special atmosphere, its spirit, its hidden beauty.

Regarding his stories, he wrote in a letter to a friend: "There is no fiction in my story. I had to go through and experience all this. The names of the characters are changed. Some of them are no longer with us."

On May 24, 1941, the writer was arrested and transported to the city of Kirov. On December 14, 1941, he was shot for "publishing books, pamphlets, and plays of a slanderous, anti-Soviet nature." He was rehabilitated in 1991.

* "Precentor" is an approximate translation of the Russian term псаломщик (psalomshchik). In 19th and early 20th century Russia, this minor cleric was the musical and administrative mainstay of the parish. He would regulate the singing and reading in the choir, coordinate the order of worship, and also functioned as the parish clerk, maintaining all church records and handling the parish's official correspondence.
